I passed the National Entrance Exam for Sainik School Tilaiya in 2009.
I was eleven.
After the written test came an interview with the Headmaster, Principal, and Registrar - all officers posted from the Indian armed forces.
They asked me about IPL: which teams would be playing tonight, who I liked.
I said I enjoy playing sports rather than watching them.
I don't remember if that was the right answer.
I got in anyway.

Then came the medical test.
Pretty invasive by any civilian standard.
One common reason people failed was knocking knees - when you stand, your knees touch each other.
Apparently that's bad for your ability to run.
The military cares about these things.
Civilian schools ask if you have allergies.

## The Sorting Hat of Tilaiya

After the entrance formalities, you were assigned a House.
The process was akin to the Sorting Hat in Harry Potter, except instead of a sentient hat, it was Hostel Superintendents asking inane questions and slotting you based on your characteristics.
I got Vikram House.
Vikram had a tradition of Hockey and Drill, which meant everyone in the house had to dedicate at least some attention to hockey.
Usually, it was much more than "some."

## Class 6: The Honeymoon

Class 6 lived away from the other classes.
We used to joke that the first year is the honeymoon period of the stay at SST.
And it was.
We transformed our dormitory area completely.
Got some swans, ducks, and fishes in old broken boats.
Made a garden.
We even kept rabbits in a cage, and quickly learnt that jackals and foxes and cats can dig their way in.
That rabbit gave birth to twelve children and six of them died in a week.

But we saved a lot of life too.
The fishes were having a blast.
Until the evening when we all returned from dinner with some chicken and everybody brought some for the fish.
It was too much overall for them.
A few died of overeating.
We were eleven-year-olds running a small ecosystem with zero training and enormous enthusiasm.
The outcomes were predictable.

## The System and Its Design Flaws

I was kicked out of the hockey team by Suraj Ji in Class 8.
Then I was on the School Team in Class 9 - not because I got better at hockey, but because the system needed cultural talent for inter-school delegations, and the selection mechanism was too rigid to separate sports from cultural participation.
I found my way in through a design flaw.
That is a very real-world lesson about how institutions work, and I learnt it at fourteen.[^1]

[^1]: This is a pattern I've since seen everywhere - in university admissions, in hiring, in grant applications.
    Systems designed for one purpose get repurposed for another, and the people who notice the seams do better than those who assume the system works as advertised.

Academically, I started at rank 19 out of 34 in Class 6.
By Class 11, I was Best Academician.
Each jump coincided precisely with dropping a language subject.
First Sanskrit, then Hindi.
My brain apparently had a fixed budget for languages, and it was already overdrawn.[^2]

[^2]: I've written about my complicated relationship with languages in [another post](/languages).

## School Captain

Somewhere between Class 6 and Class 12, I went from admiring the School Captain - Vikas Ji, who seemed impossibly composed - to becoming one myself.
The weight of it hit immediately.
You are a student among officers and an officer among students.
An impossible middle position that a teenager is not equipped to hold, but holds anyway because the system demands it.

I skipped breakfast most mornings to avoid complaints.
That was my governance strategy: if you are not there, no one can complain to you.
Looking back, this was both endearing and a little concerning as a leadership approach.
But it worked.

Life of a School Captain is not much less busy than a Mayor of a small town.
Aw was the common adage, "an officer among the students and a student among the officers".
But it taught me a lot: especially how to be objective and neutral.[^3]
I learnt what is leadership by example and it definitely improved the state of the school.

[^3]: Whenever I'm sort of luck and end up as an adjudicator, I say "I'm from from Science's side. Neither of you".
    Like Ranchordas Shamardas Chanchad (Rancho) from 3 Idiots.

One of the first things you receive as a Class 6 kid is a List of Manners - norms and recommendations derived from Officer Like Qualities, the 15 axioms upon which the SSB Board judges candidates for the Indian armed forces.
You read them as rules.
Years later, you realise they were trying to build a certain kind of person.

I also became obsessed with maintaining a diary.
I documented everything - schedules, events, observations.
That instinct for systematic documentation has never left me.
It shows up now in how I build course syllabi, maintain research notes, write these blog posts.[^4]

[^4]: My Vice-Captain, Sudhanshu, got suspended a week after assuming the role.
    That is a whole story I'm not telling here.

## The Saturday Night Movies

There was a tradition at SST.
Saturday night movies in the school assembly hall.
After the movie finished, you stayed.
In Class 6, we were often sent early for dinner.
In Class 7-8, a little later.
Class 9-10, last.

When I became School Captain, I stopped the practice.
Not because I had ideological objections to seniority, but because making kids wait an extra thirty minutes after a movie for no reason seemed pointless.
